# Exercise 3: A known field of view (PR113)

You will measure a spot that crosses a video in about a tenth of a second, then tell mcdonald two
things about the picture: what the camera's own angle marks say, and how much the picture was
stretched. It then gives how wide the camera sees, how fast the spot turns across the view, and
what its speed would be at each distance.

- **Time:** about 10 minutes.
- **Space:** a 67 MB download, and about 43 MB for the frames you open.
- **You need:** mcdonald 0.4.2 or newer, installed and set up ([Install](../../README.md#install)).

## The video

PR113 is three minutes of video, which the record says was likely filmed by a US military infrared
camera in 1996. In it, "an area of contrast transits the sensor field-of-view,
entering near the upper right corner and exiting near the lower left corner of the frame", at
about 0:14. You can read the whole record on
[its DVIDS page](https://www.dvidshub.net/video/1014119). You will look at two seconds of it, from
0:12.63 to 0:14.63.

The camera draws lines with numbers on them across the picture: its angle marks, a ladder of
lines 5 degrees apart that tilts as the aircraft banks. Read up the picture, one degree spans 36.25
pixels on the screen. An earlier analysis of this video read about 35.4, treated the pixels as
square, and so took the picture's 1,920 pixels to span about 54 degrees.

But the pixels are not square. The video is a 1996 television tape, a picture 720 pixels wide by
480 tall in the old 4:3 shape, blown up to fill a 1,920 by 1,080 frame. A 4:3 picture 1,080 tall
is only 1,440 wide, so each pixel has been drawn 1.333 times wider than it is tall (1,920 over
1,440). The angle marks say how much angle a pixel covers up the picture; across it, a pixel
covers a third more. You will give mcdonald both numbers: the marks' 36.25 pixels a degree, and the
stretch of 1.333. (mcdonald's own reading of the stretch, from how the ground turns in the picture
as the aircraft banks, is 1.29, give or take 0.07: the same number.)

## In the window

1. Start the window: type `mcdonald-gui` in a terminal, or use the mcdonald icon if
   `mcdonald setup` added one.
2. On the first page, press **Open by catalog name…**, type `PR113`, and press **OK**.
3. mcdonald asks whether to download PR113 from DVIDS. Press **Yes**.
4. The page **Select the video segment** opens. Click in the box beside **Set start**, type
   `380` and press Enter. Then click in the box beside **Set end**, type `440` and press Enter.
   The line under the boxes should say 0:12.63 to 0:14.63. The spot is there for only four frames,
   408 to 411, so it is easy to miss when the video plays. Leave
   **How many objects are you looking for?** at **I don't know**, and press
   **Open this segment**.
5. mcdonald saves those frames as pictures, with a bar on the right that shows how far it has
   got.
6. In the **Measure the object** box on the right, press **Begin**. Three lights under the
   button show the steps: **Find**, **Follow** and **Measure**. Nothing is asked on the way.
   This takes about 3 minutes.
7. The report appears on the right, under the button. Its first line gives a speed in pixels a
   second: a speed on the screen. Under it is a card called **Measure the sound**, which is
   another exercise, so you can leave it for now.
8. Now give what you know of the picture. The angle marks and the stretch are not among the
   plain questions under the report, so press **Advanced Mode** (the button under the box) and
   then **Measure** (the third step). Open the fold **Provide additional information about this
   video (optional)**. In the box **graticule scale (px/deg) → k**, type `36.25`. In the box
   **pixel aspect a = width / height**, type `1.333,a 4:3 tape shown in 16:9`: the number, a
   comma, and in your own words where it came from. Press **Measure** (it may say **Measure
   again**). It shows the track sheet and asks whether the track is on the object in every frame:
   press **Yes, on the object in every frame**. About 3 minutes later the first line has changed,
   and new rows are on the card.

## On the command line

These are the same steps, for a terminal or an AI agent. First, find what moves in those frames:

```bash
mcdonald look PR113 --n0 380 --n1 440 --propose
```

This downloads PR113 the first time, saves the frames, and looks for things that move against the
background. It takes about 2 minutes. It lists 7 things, best first, and draws them in the picture
`pr113/segment-380-440/pr113_look_proposals_380_440.png`. All seven are "weak": Find is not sure of
any of them, so look at the picture yourself. Thing 1 is the dark spot seen on frames 408 to 411,
moving 141 pixels each frame: the one the record describes.

Under the list, it prints a line that starts `to take one:`. Here it is, shortened:

```text
to take one:  mcdonald mark PR113 --no-window --link --set object@408=1007.9,317.3 --set object@411=700.9,605.6 --why "proposal 1 of 7 from look --propose (…); I looked at its strip and it is the object because ..." --segment 380-440
```

Copy the command (everything after `to take one:`), replace the `...` after "because" with your own
reason, and run it. Its `--segment 380-440` puts the marks in that segment's folder,
`pr113/segment-380-440/`, beside the picture: each segment of a video has a folder of its own.
It should say "4 of 4 frames linked, 408–411". Marks set this way are recorded as an agent's
choice, with your reason, and the report says so. That is expected.

Then measure it, over the same frames. This takes about 2 minutes:

```bash
mcdonald run PR113 --marks pr113/segment-380-440/pr113_marks.json --n0 380 --n1 440 --skip integrity
```

And give it the angle marks' scale and the picture's stretch, which the window's Measure form
takes. This takes a few seconds, and nothing is measured again:

```bash
mcdonald report pr113/segment-380-440/pr113_case.json --graticule 36.25 --pixel-aspect "1.333,a 4:3 tape shown in 16:9"
```

To have an AI agent do it, you can give it this prompt:

> Please use the mcdonald toolkit to analyze the PR113 video released under PURSUE, following
> https://github.com/haqqmisra/mcdonald/blob/main/docs/exercises/03-pr113.md. Run
> `mcdonald readme` to get started.

## What you should get

Both ways give the same conclusion and the same numbers. Before you give the marks and the
stretch, the report starts:

> **No physical conclusion.** The object moved about 4269 pixels a second in the image, and its real speed and size cannot be found from this video alone.

After you give them, it starts:

> **Indeterminate, range needed.** The object moved about 103° a second in the image and is 21 pixels across in the file (0.43–0.58°: the picture is drawn 1.333 times wider than tall); what it is depends on how far away it is: as a bird (0.2–2 m) it would be 20–260 m away and moving at least 36–470 m/s across the line of sight; as a party balloon or a small drone (0.3–1 m), 30–130 m away and 53–240 m/s; as a weather balloon (2 m), 200–260 m away and 360–470 m/s; as a balloon cluster (2–5 m), 200–660 m away and 360–1,200 m/s; as a tethered aerostat (5–20 m), 490 m–2.6 km away and 890–4,700 m/s; as a fighter jet (17 m), 1.7 km–2.2 km away and 3,000–4,000 m/s.

The window's report card also says it followed the spot on 4 frames (frames 408 to 411), at
142.3 pixels a frame. After you give the marks and the stretch, new rows are added: **Field of
view** 39.7° by 29.8° up, **Scale** 2077 px/rad, **Pixels, wide to tall** 1.333 wide to tall, and **Turn rate**
1.80 rad/s. The question on the card changes to "Its real speed needs the distance to the object".

## What it means

- **From pixels to angles.** Once mcdonald knows how much angle a pixel covers, it can turn
  pixels into angles. The marks give the scale up the picture; the stretch says a pixel across
  the picture covers a third less. The spot moves 142 pixels a frame in the file, down and to the
  left at about 45 degrees; in square pixels, with the stretch taken out, that is about 125 square pixels a frame,
  and at 36.25 pixels a degree it turns across the view at about 103 degrees a second
  (1.80 rad/s): it crosses the whole picture in well under half a second. Read with one scale and
  square pixels, as the earlier analysis did, the same track gives about 121 degrees a second, and a
  field of 54 degrees where the picture is really about 39 degrees across by 30 up.
- **A speed still needs a distance.** How fast something turns across your view depends on how
  far away it is: a fly near your face and a plane far off can turn across it at the same rate.
  The speed is the turn rate times the distance. The record gives no distance, so the first line
  says **Indeterminate, range needed**.
- **What it would be at each distance.** The first line puts three things where their sizes
  would be. A bird would be 18 to 180 meters away. A 2-meter weather balloon would have to be
  about 180 meters away, going about 400 meters a second: faster than sound. A 17-meter fighter
  jet would be about 1.5 kilometers away, going about 3,500 meters a second. Unless the spot was
  small and close, it was very fast. The speeds are the least it could be going at each
  distance: they count only the motion across your view. Press **Full report** and find the
  picture called "Size (left) and speed (right) against distance": its one line shows the same
  thing at every distance.
- **Try a distance.** Press **Change** on the card, type `3` in **Distance to the object** with
  **kilometers** beside it, and press **Work out the speed**. The first line now says
  **Supersonic motion**: at 3 kilometers the spot is about 23 to 30 meters across (two numbers,
  because the picture's stretch leaves open whether its 21 pixels were counted across or up),
  about a fighter jet's size, and moving at least 5,400 meters a second across your view. That
  is the least it could be going, so it is faster than sound however it moved toward or away
  from the camera. Try `0.1` (100 meters): about 0.8 to 1 meter across and at least 180 meters
  a second, under the speed of sound, and the first line says **Indeterminate, angle needed**,
  because motion toward or away from the camera could still make it faster. The record gives no
  distance; this only shows what one would tell you.
- **Why not 54 degrees.** The earlier analysis gave a turn rate of 2.10 radians a second, about
  121 degrees a second, and a field of 54 degrees, from the same marks and the same track, with
  the pixels taken as square. You can see that number yourself:
  `mcdonald report pr113/segment-380-440/pr113_case.json --graticule 35.4 --forget pixel-aspect`
  gives 2.10. The difference is the stretch: the spot's motion across the picture is counted in
  pixels that are a third wider than the ones the marks were read in. The 142 pixels a frame is
  still right as a count of the file's pixels; it is the turn into degrees that needs the stretch.
  Where did 1.333 come from? Two places that agree: the shape of the tape's picture (a 4:3 picture
  filling a 16:9 frame), and the ground itself, which turns in the picture as the aircraft banks
  and does so as a picture stretched by 1.29, give or take 0.07, would. The camera's own drawn
  symbols are stretched more, 1.75 wide to tall, which is the display's own doing and not the
  picture's: give `--pixel-aspect 1.753,symbols` and the rate comes out about 94 degrees a second,
  which is what the symbols' stretch would say if it were the picture's. The report records which
  number you gave and where you said it came from.
- **Check the track by eye.** The report card says **Track not yet checked by eye**. Press
  **Full report** and find the track sheet: a small picture of each frame, with a ring where
  mcdonald followed the spot. If the ring is on the spot in all four frames, press **I looked**.
  On the command line, open `pr113/segment-380-440/pr113_all_frames.jpg`, then run
  `mcdonald report pr113/segment-380-440/pr113_case.json --i-looked`.
